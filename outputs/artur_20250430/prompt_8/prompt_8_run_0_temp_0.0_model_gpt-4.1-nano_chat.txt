To conduct a robust LCA comparing battery recycling and reuse in energy storage, especially given the lack of direct Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) data for reuse and the rapid evolution of recycling technologies, a comprehensive and transparent methodological strategy is essential.

**Handling Data Uncertainty and Technological Variability:**  
Given the scarcity of specific reuse data, I would adopt a hybrid approach combining literature review, expert elicitation, and proxy data from similar technologies (e.g., second-life applications of EV batteries). To address uncertainty, Monte Carlo simulations or sensitivity analyses can be employed, varying key parameters such as lifespan, efficiency, and material recovery rates (Bare et al., 2019). This probabilistic approach quantifies the impact of data variability, providing a range of possible environmental impacts rather than a single deterministic value. Additionally, scenario analysis can explore different technological pathways—e.g., advanced recycling methods versus emerging reuse strategies—to capture potential future developments (Hawkins et al., 2020).

**Setting System Boundaries and Choosing a Functional Unit:**  
System boundaries should encompass all relevant life cycle stages: raw material extraction, manufacturing, use phase, and end-of-life management (recycling or reuse). For reuse, boundaries must include the additional manufacturing or refurbishment steps, as well as the extended use phase, to accurately reflect environmental impacts. For recycling, boundaries should include collection, processing, and material recovery. The functional unit should be defined as "per kWh of energy storage capacity delivered over the battery’s entire functional lifespan," aligning with common energy storage assessments (Dunn et al., 2015). This ensures comparability by normalizing impacts to the service provided, regardless of whether the battery is reused or recycled.

**Justification of Methodological Assumptions:**  
To communicate assumptions to stakeholders unfamiliar with LCA, I would explicitly document all choices, such as the assumed lifespan extension for reused batteries, efficiency of recycling processes, and the boundaries set for each scenario. I would justify these assumptions based on current literature, expert opinion, and industry reports, emphasizing their role in managing uncertainty and enabling fair comparison (ISO 14044, 2006). For example, if assuming a certain lifespan extension for reused batteries, I would cite recent studies demonstrating potential performance metrics (Li et al., 2021). Transparency ensures stakeholders understand the basis of the results and the importance of assumptions in interpreting the findings.

In summary, this strategy combines probabilistic methods, clear boundary setting, and transparent assumptions to produce a credible, comparable LCA despite data limitations and technological uncertainties, facilitating informed decision-making in battery end-of-life management (Weidema et al., 2018).
