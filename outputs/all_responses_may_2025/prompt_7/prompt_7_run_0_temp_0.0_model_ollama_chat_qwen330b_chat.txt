This approach is critical for process optimization and stakeholder transparency, as it avoids speculative assumptions about market or grid-level changes (Guinée et al., 2002). **Methodology**: Use **consequential LCA**.  
**Justification**: In renewable grids, the hydrogen process may influence energy demand patterns, grid stability, and resource allocation. Consequential LCA captures system-level impacts, such as shifts in renewable energy use or competition for minerals (e.g., electrolyzer materials). **3. Large-Scale Deployment in Fossil-Dominated Grids**  
**Methodology**: Use **consequential LCA**.  
**Justification**: Here, hydrogen production could displace fossil fuels, but consequential LCA accounts for indirect effects, such as changes in energy mix or rebound effects (e.g., increased demand for electrolysis). This method is essential for evaluating fossil resource use reduction and potential trade-offs (e.g., mineral scarcity from scaling up electrolyzers). For example, if hydrogen replaces coal, consequential LCA would quantify avoided fossil emissions but also highlight increased mineral extraction (Bauer et al., 2014). Policymakers and industry stakeholders require this to balance climate goals with resource constraints.  

**Key Considerations**:  
- **Attributional LCA** is suitable for process-specific, data-rich scenarios (e.g., pilot-scale) where system interactions are minimal.  
- **Consequential LCA** is critical for large-scale scenarios, as it addresses market and system-level changes, aligning with stakeholder interests in systemic sustainability.  
- Both methods inform the company’s goals: attributional for operational efficiency, consequential for strategic impact assessment.
